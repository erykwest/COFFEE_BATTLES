Field Manual / Battle resilience

Morale, Energy & Rout

Winning a clash does not always mean destroying every enemy soldier. Often the decisive result is the moment a formation can no longer hold together.

Three things the unit bar is telling you

A regiment has health (V), energy (E) and a mental resilience resource called Brain (B) in the current battle UI. They are not interchangeable. Health falls from damage and losses; energy is spent on movement and fighting; mental resilience is reduced by shock, losses and destabilising battlefield events.

This means you can have a unit with many soldiers left that still becomes ineffective. It may be too exhausted to manoeuvre, shaken by repeated blows, or caught in a rout. The enemy is trying to undermine the whole formation, not just win a contest of remaining health points.

Energy rewards pacing

Movement consumes energy and fighting consumes more. Standing still lets a regiment recover it. The game begins units with an energy pool and reduces their combat performance when it runs out; in the current rule set, a regiment at zero energy cannot move and cannot fight effectively. A long march followed immediately by a charge can therefore turn a theoretically powerful unit into an underperformer at the decisive moment.

Think of energy as a timetable. If the flanking cavalry must reach the enemy side before the centre collapses, it needs both a valid route and remaining energy. If infantry races across the map only to meet a fresh defending line, it may lose the exchange without being the weaker unit on paper.

Morale changes in stages

The battle rules describe four morale states: STEADY, SHAKEN, WAVERING and ROUT. Steady units still have most of their B resource; shaken and wavering regiments suffer combat penalties; at zero B a unit routs and stops fighting under normal command. The key transition is not purely visual: once a unit routs, its behaviour is no longer that of a disciplined line regiment.

Shock may arrive through losing men quickly, through contact on the flank or rear, or through being hit by a strong charge. A cavalry charge therefore has a purpose beyond immediate physical damage: it can make a formation stop functioning while much of its health remains intact. That is why the setup of the charge—the angle, available path and target orientation—matters so much.

A rout can spread

The current rules impose extra shock on allied units near a rout. A fleeing allied formation can also disturb a friendly unit it physically hits from the side or rear. So even an unbroken unit may be affected by the sudden failure of its neighbour. This creates a recognisable battlefield cascade: one regiment breaks, another suffers a shock penalty, and the line loses coherence just as the enemy presses forward.

It is possible to lose an army by letting several badly stressed groups touch each other at the wrong moment. A compact wall of troops has advantages, but packing every unit into the same small escape route can magnify losses when something goes wrong. Keep withdrawal routes open and consider a second line that can intervene without obstructing a retreat.

Voluntary retreat versus uncontrolled rout

A voluntary Retreat is a command: the group attempts to break contact and put distance between itself and a threat. A ROUT is a morale failure and the fleeing regiment no longer behaves as an obedient combat unit. The game also includes pursuit behaviour and situations in which a routed group may recover if it escapes danger.

The timing of your withdrawal changes the outcome. If you wait until morale is almost empty, there may be no time to reorganise. Pulling one battered regiment back while a fresher unit holds the enemy can conserve your battle line. Retreating every regiment simultaneously, however, may surrender the only position preventing a wider collapse.

Observe the whole army, not one duel

The strategic victory bar records the share of the opposing force's points lost through destruction or flight. Reaching the collapse threshold can trigger an army-wide rout. The match therefore has two levels of decision-making: local contact, where you try to win a tactical exchange, and overall army stability, where losing too many points leads to broader failure.

In your next battle, watch the unit's remaining energy and morale before a charge rather than only after. Note whether the first regiment to rout was isolated, badly oriented or exhausted. Then restart with one changed order. Small controlled experiments reveal more than changing the whole army at once.
