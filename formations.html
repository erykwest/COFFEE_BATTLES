Formations & Facing

A regiment is an oriented shape, not a point. Its front can fight one way; its flank and rear tell a very different story.

Three formations, three different purposes

The current build uses three principal formations. Base is a 3U-wide, 2U-deep formation; Column is 2U wide and 3U deep; Line is 6U wide and 1U deep. Changing formation takes two seconds and is allowed during an engagement. These dimensions make frontage an explicit decision rather than a purely visual decoration.

When to use each one

Base is a flexible starting point. Its width is modest enough for normal movement, while its depth makes it less cumbersome than an extended line when the route bends. If you are uncertain about a regiment's next assignment, base formation avoids making a strong early commitment to attack frontage or transit width.

Column is about access. A narrow bridge or passage can prevent a six-unit-wide line from reaching the useful fighting position. The column lets troops navigate a tighter route, but it brings a narrower front into contact. It should therefore be treated as a transit shape rather than automatically the best way to deliver maximum melee pressure.

Line widens the frontage. Against an enemy that can be met head-on along a broad axis, a greater share of the regiment can potentially participate. Yet its width can become a liability near obstacles, and the exposed line needs protection from opponents attacking beyond its ends. A wide unit that turns toward one threat can uncover another.

Contact frontage is not just nominal width

The battle model distinguishes the declared front (F) from the front actually in contact (Fc). If only part of your line overlaps the enemy, you cannot assume the whole advertised frontage participates. As units move and align, their effective contact may change. This is why a good charge angle or a well-positioned second regiment can matter more than having the widest formation on paper.

Picture a 6U line approaching a much narrower target diagonally. At the first moment of contact, only a portion may overlap. A straightened position and a coordinated approach let more of the line do work. Conversely, two units trying to enter the same narrow gap can obstruct one another even if they have more total health and combat rating.

Front, flank and rear

Facing controls the value of an engagement. A frontal clash asks both sides to use their fighting front. A flank or rear contact adds a tactical penalty for the defender and extra psychological shock. In the current combat rules, a flank adds +2 shock and a rear adds +3 in the ordinary engagement context; charge interactions can create still larger effects.

Units do not rotate instantly. They must spend time turning, and a unit already occupied in front cannot always meet a second threat before it connects. The practical lesson is simple: pin with one group, attack the side with another. There is a cost: your flanking regiment needs travel time, a safe route and enough energy left to exploit the opening.

Practical experiment: line versus column

  1. Deploy two comparable infantry regiments in open ground. Start in Base and observe a frontal engagement.
  2. Try again with the attacker in Column. Compare how soon its leading troops establish contact and how wide that contact becomes.
  3. Try once more with a Line, but place a blocker or obstacle along one side. Watch how path choice changes the benefit of the extra width.

There is no universally correct formation. Terrain determines which formations can reach an approach, morale and energy determine how long they can use it, and intelligent facing can make a smaller force win an apparently unfavourable exchange.
