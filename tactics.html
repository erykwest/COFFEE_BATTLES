Field Manual / Six practice drills

Tactical Scenarios

Tactics become easier to learn when each rematch changes one decision instead of ten. These six exercises work with the game's existing modes and unit roster.

1. Pin the front, hit the flank

Goal: understand the difference between winning a fight with numbers and winning through geometry. Select a mostly open map, place a central infantry regiment opposite a comparable enemy and keep a second fast group on one side. Start by letting the main lines engage. Only after contact is established, send the spare group toward the enemy's exposed flank.

What to observe: does the defender begin to turn, is its side still exposed when the second unit arrives, and does morale collapse faster than in a frontal-only attempt? Replay once with the flanking unit committed too early. It may arrive before the target is fixed in place and simply face a prepared enemy front.

2. Delay a cavalry charge

Goal: make charge timing measurable. Build a force with cavalry and a line unit. First issue an immediate cavalry charge into an intact enemy front. Note the impact and the cavalry's remaining energy. Then restart and let your infantry occupy the defender before the cavalry moves into an open side.

What to observe: cavalry's role is not only speed. Its charge shock is valuable when the opponent cannot easily reorient. On difficult ground, the run-up may be longer than expected, especially if forests block the direct route.

3. The reserve is not wasted points

Goal: practise replacing a damaged regiment without losing the whole line. Deploy two frontline groups and keep a third immediately behind the more vulnerable sector. Allow the forward unit to become tired or shaken, then withdraw it while moving the reserve to maintain contact.

What to observe: whether the retreat path is open, whether the reserve is close enough to arrive promptly, and whether the withdrawing unit obstructs its replacement. Repeat with all three regiments packed tightly at the front to see why a reserve needs physical space, not merely an army-list slot.

4. Keep the archers alive

Goal: understand shooting range versus direct combat risk. Use infantry to screen archers or crossbowmen. Place the ranged group far enough back that it is not the first target, but close enough to acquire a visible enemy. Compare a safe, deliberate firing position with a second run where the ranged troops are allowed to approach too far forward.

What to observe: the archers' longer reach compared with crossbowmen's armour-piercing profile, the effect of walls or buildings on line of sight, and the moment a melee group must move to prevent the shooters being caught. Damage per volley is not the whole story if the firing unit has to stop shooting to survive.

5. Cross the river without a traffic jam

Goal: test the cost of a constrained approach. On a River map, order several regiments toward one bridge. Watch the congestion and the actual width available when the first unit meets resistance. Then replay with the force split between legal crossings or with narrower column transit where appropriate.

What to observe: which regiment reaches useful contact first, how much of the following army is still waiting, and whether changing formation improves transit but weakens the first melee. The lesson is not that a second crossing is always faster, but that an army needs more than one workable route.

6. Win by breaking morale

Goal: stop treating health bars as the only victory signal. Concentrate on one poorly supported enemy group rather than spreading damage evenly. Use a strong contact and an open flank to generate losses and shock. Watch nearby enemy regiments when the first target routs.

What to observe: how a local rout can increase pressure on allies, how fleeing regiments interact with friendly formations and how the strategic victory bar tracks destroyed or exited army points. Retry with your own force more spread out and compare whether its morale is more resistant to a cascade.

How to keep the experiment fair

Use the same point budget, comparable unit groups and similar ground when testing a single mechanic. Mirror mode is useful for understanding symmetrical matchups, while Custom mode offers more control over both armies. The opponent's movement and tactical decisions can still create variation, so do not draw conclusions from only one result.

For each exercise, write down three observations: the first decisive contact, the first serious morale change and the reason the battle ended. These short notes are more useful than a raw win/loss number, because they teach you how to reproduce an advantage—or avoid a repeated mistake.
