Units & Army Building

A good army is not a collection of expensive regiments. It is a plan for how troops will pin, protect, shoot and exploit a gap.

The eight current regiment types

The game's unit catalogue assigns each regiment a point cost, health (V), combat rating (C), armour (AR) and, for shooters, a weapon range measured in battlefield units (U). These values come from the current game definition. Ranged profiles use an additional aim value and different firing intervals, so range alone does not measure damage output.

The front line: infantry, heavy infantry and spearmen

Infantry is the low-complexity reference unit: 100 points, moderate defence and a straightforward combat role. It is useful when you need several groups to cover a wide front. Two ordinary infantry groups may protect more space and offer more tactical flexibility than a single expensive regiment, but they also demand coordination.

Heavy infantry costs more and marches more slowly. It uses armour 2, making it the obvious candidate for positions likely to absorb repeated contact. The danger is operational rather than statistical: a slow unit committed to the wrong road or flank may arrive after the decisive fight is over.

Spearmen also have armour 2 and possess a front-cover advantage in the current unit profile. They reward facing and disciplined placement. Put them where they can receive an attack from the expected direction; the advantage is less useful if an opponent simply goes around their side.

The screen: light infantry

Light infantry is inexpensive, faster on the march and can fire at short range. Its armour is zero. Use it to probe forward ground, screen a more valuable formation and exploit routes that do not demand sustained face-to-face melee. It is a poor candidate to hold a narrow bridge alone against a heavier group, especially if it has no path of withdrawal.

The fire base: archers and crossbowmen

Archers reach 15U and fire relatively frequently; crossbowmen reach 13U, fire more slowly and have armour piercing in the current profile. Neither has a strong melee combat score. The choice is not simply "longer range versus shorter range": crossbows trade rate of fire and reach for a different hit profile against protected targets.

Put ranged groups behind a line that can keep enemy melee troops away. Before issuing an attack, check whether the intended target is visible and whether terrain blocks the line of sight. If a group walks toward a target to acquire range, it may expose itself; manual positioning is often safer than leaving every decision to automatic engagement.

The mobile wing: cavalry and light cavalry

Cavalry has combat 3, armour 3 and a strong charge-shock multiplier. Its effectiveness comes from timing and an open approach; spending its burst against a well-presented frontal defence gives up much of its manoeuvre advantage. Mounted units cannot enter forests in the current rules, so the shortest-looking path is not always legal.

Light cavalry moves quickly and can fire out to 10U. It is an instrument for screening, drawing attention and pressuring exposed troops rather than a substitute for heavy cavalry in melee. It is particularly useful when the opponent has committed its main line but has not protected the approaches to its rear.

Build around a job, not a shopping list

Start by deciding what each regiment will do: anchor a position, screen the approach, shoot from safety or strike a flank. A force containing only anchors struggles to exploit openings; a force containing only mobile troops may struggle to hold ground. The deployment phase is the first test of whether your list contains complementary jobs.

Try a practical comparison at equal point budgets: one army with a stronger central line, then one army with a lighter centre and an extra mobile regiment. Replay similar ground in Mirror or Custom mode. Note when the flanking unit arrives, how much damage the centre has already taken and whether the result depended on positioning rather than purely on unit cost.
