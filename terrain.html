Terrain, Sight & Fog of War

The battlefield is not a picture behind the units. It is a network of routes, obstacles, cover and information gaps that determines which orders are possible.

Choose terrain with the same care as troops

Coffee Battles lets you choose a map type including plains, hills, valleys, rivers, forests, mountains, villages and sea, or allow the game to select terrain randomly. The same army composition can behave very differently on these maps: open ground rewards manoeuvre, obstacles create chokepoints, and difficult lines of sight can take away a ranged group's best targets.

Start map planning with two questions: where can my regiments actually travel? and where can they see or engage the enemy? These are separate questions. A route may be passable but expose your line to a flanking attack, while an attractive firing position may fail because an intervening building blocks visibility.

Rivers, bridges and fords

In the current rules, rivers are crossed at bridges and fords rather than anywhere along their length. This concentrates movement into a limited number of approaches. The temptation is to move every regiment toward the nearest crossing, but doing so can create a queue; a unit stuck behind friendly troops is contributing little to the actual battle.

Instead, assign jobs: one regiment secures the approach, another supports it, and a third searches for an alternative route. A narrow crossing can favour a sturdy defender if the attacker enters in an unsuitable formation. On the other hand, a defender that watches only one bridge may lose the flank if an enemy finds a second crossing.

Forests and mounted troops

Mounted regiments are blocked from forests in the published unit profiles. This is not merely a speed modifier. A planned cavalry thrust that cuts straight through woodland may be impossible, forcing a longer route around the edge. The resulting arrival time could completely change when a flank attack should begin.

Forests also change the visibility problem. A hidden or partly obscured approach can help keep the defender uncertain, but it can interfere with your own shooters' line of sight. Use foot regiments to occupy or screen forest approaches and reserve the cavalry for routes it can legally use.

Buildings, mountains and hard barriers

Buildings and mountains act as solid obstacles in the current navigation rules. They are useful defensive anchors because they reduce the directions from which a regiment can be reached. But they can also box you in, prevent a retreat or force a broad formation into an awkward turn.

When defending a village approach, hold the spaces between obstacles rather than attempting to place a unit inside every structure. Ensure the second line has room to rotate and move. When attacking, do not commit mobile troops to a flank that turns out to be blocked; scout or inspect the ground before the main contact begins.

Line of sight and last-known enemy positions

Each side sees the battlefield through its units. The current fog-of-war layer distinguishes enemies that are visible, last known and unknown. A seen unit may disappear from live view when it leaves observation or passes behind an obstacle. The game can retain a marker at the place it was last seen; that marker is intelligence, not a guarantee that the enemy is still standing there.

The current rule guide describes a typical observation distance of about 20U for a unit. A ranged unit still needs the right firing range and a valid line of sight to attack its chosen target. Losing vision can disrupt an otherwise good plan. A scout-like screen or a forward infantry group can be valuable simply because it keeps an enemy formation observed while the main force moves.

How to test a route

  1. Choose River terrain and send two groups toward the same crossing. Notice how route width and order timing matter.
  2. Repeat with one group diverted to another passage. Compare its arrival time with the congestion avoided.
  3. On a Forest map, order a mounted unit toward a destination beyond the trees. Watch the detour instead of assuming a direct march.
  4. In a Village map, try keeping your ranged line farther behind the buildings, then move one observer forward and inspect whether targets become visible.

Good terrain play is not about finding a permanent "best spot." It is about knowing what the map prevents and arranging your troops so that the enemy faces those constraints while you preserve options.
